Elian hid the seed in a tea tin beneath his bed, which was the sort of decision that seemed intelligent only at three in the morning.

By breakfast, the tin had grown warm. By midday, a hairline root had pushed through its steel seam. When Elian returned from the orchard, he found a green shoot curling toward the cabin light and a thin line of condensation running down the wall.

He reached for it and the room vanished.

Rain struck a red roof. Someone—an old woman whose hands he somehow recognized—was laughing as she dragged baskets beneath an awning. Beyond her, trees climbed a mountainside in impossible disorder. No pruning grids. No numbered beds. No ceiling.

The memory released him with the taste of pears on his tongue.

“Remarkable,” said a voice behind him.

Archivist Sera Quill stood in the open doorway. Her silver uniform carried no rank marks, only the black thread worn by those entrusted with the ship’s official past. She looked from the ruptured tin to the damp wall, then to Elian.

“You disabled an agricultural recorder last night,” she said. “You also produced thirty-seven seconds of neural activity matching no simulation in the ship’s library.”

Elian moved between her and the seed. It was not a brave gesture. Sera was seventy, slight as folded paper, and entirely unarmed. Yet everyone aboard knew that archivists could erase a family’s ration history with a sentence.

“Are you here to arrest me?” he asked.

“I’m here because I have dreamed of that same rain every night for forty-one years.”

The shoot unfolded its first leaf.

Across its surface, in veins dark as ink, appeared a map of a coastline the ship’s records claimed had never existed.
